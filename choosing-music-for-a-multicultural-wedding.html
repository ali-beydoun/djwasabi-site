Wedding music

How do we choose music for a multicultural wedding?

I'd start with the traditions that matter to each of you, then plan how they fit alongside the music you both love.

By Wassim, DJ Wasabi
30 October 2026

In this guide

Your traditionsDances and recordingsFamily requestsEntrances and performersIntroductions

If your families listen to different music, you might be wondering how to give everyone something they'll enjoy. I'd start by asking what matters to each of you. There may be a dance your family is looking forward to, or a song you'd love your parents to hear.

Tell me what you enjoy together too. We can make room for those family moments alongside the music you'd choose for yourselves.

Which traditions would you like included?

You might want Lebanese dabke and Greek dancing at the same reception. Another couple might want a few songs from one family's background and mostly contemporary music for the rest of the night. I'd ask you both before putting a plan together. Whatever your backgrounds, you can choose which traditions you want as part of your wedding.

If you're unsure what a parent means by a particular tradition, ask them to explain it while we're planning. Find out which part matters to them and decide together whether you'd like to include it. You can also tell me about anything you'd prefer to leave out.

Name the dance and send me the recording

Dabke is a group dance shared across the Levant, including Lebanon, Syria, Jordan and Palestine, with different local forms. If it's part of your plans, send me the songs your family enjoys and tell me who would like to lead.

For Greek dancing, kalamatianos is a circle dance, while zeibekiko is generally danced solo and improvised. If someone wants a zeibekiko moment, I'd ask who it's for and which recording they want. We can give that dancer the floor, then agree whether you'd like to invite everyone in for a different dance afterwards.

A link or short family video can help explain what you mean. Let me know if you want the full song played, especially for a particular dance, so we can allow time for it.

Agree how you'd like family requests handled

I'd ask each of you for the songs you really want included. If relatives are helping with cultural music choices, give them a chance to suggest favourites before the wedding. You can listen together and let me know what you've agreed on.

We can also talk about the balance across the evening. You may want a few dances together in one part of the night, or shorter sets at different times. I'd plan that around your guests and the reception schedule, rather than assume each background needs the same number of minutes.

Tell me how much freedom you'd like guests to have with requests on the night. If there's a song you've decided against, put it on your do-not-play list. A family contact can help clarify an unfamiliar request without pulling you away from your guests.

Talk through entrances with your performers

If you're booking a zaffe, the celebratory entrance procession often involving live drummers, introduce the performers to your DJ, MC and venue before the day. I'd want to agree who gives the start cue and where the recorded music stops or takes over.

The same applies if you've booked a singer or band. Share their timings and sound requirements so we can discuss the handover. Check the entrance route and dance-floor space with the venue, and leave time for the performance before the next speech or meal service.

Help guests follow what's happening

If I'm also MCing, I'd check names and pronunciations with you beforehand. Tell me whether a short introduction would help guests understand a dance, and whether a family member would like to lead the invitation to join in.

Let me know which languages your guests are comfortable with. If an announcement needs translating, agree who will do that before the wedding. Some moments may be for everyone to join; others may be for guests to watch and enjoy. We can make that clear when we plan the introductions.

About the dances

UNESCO's regional living heritage guide describes dabke and its local variations.

The Greek Ministry of Culture's kalamatianos entry explains the circle dance. Zeibekiko NYC describes the solo, improvised form of zeibekiko.

Planning your wedding music?

Tell me which traditions and songs you'd like included, even if you're still working out the details. We can talk through the music and any MC hosting you need.
